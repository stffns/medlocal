# Acute Abdomen — Surgical Emergency Recognition and Referral

Also known as: abdomen agudo, dolor abdominal agudo, appendicitis, apendicitis, vólvulo, intususcepción, obstrucción intestinal, perforación.

**Acute abdomen is the most common surgical emergency in children and adolescents.** A CHW cannot diagnose the specific cause — the goal is to recognise the pattern and REFER. Appendicitis alone accounts for 83 per 100,000 children/year, with peak incidence between ages 15-19. Delay in referral increases the risk of perforation, peritonitis, and death.

**When to Suspect Acute Abdomen — REFER URGENTLY if ANY of these.**
- Abdominal pain that is severe, localised, and increasing in intensity
- Pain in the right lower quadrant (fosa ilíaca derecha) — classic appendicitis location
- Pain that started around the navel and migrated to the right lower side
- Pain BEFORE vomiting (pain first, then vomiting = surgical pattern; vomiting first then pain = more likely gastroenteritis)
- Bilious (green/yellow) vomiting — suggests obstruction
- Rigid or board-like abdomen (abdomen rígido, en tabla) — suggests peritonitis
- Guarding: the patient tenses the abdominal muscles when you press gently
- Rebound tenderness: pain worse when you RELEASE pressure, not when you press
- Fever + localised abdominal pain + vomiting — appendicitis until proven otherwise
- Blood in stool (hematochezia) with abdominal pain
- Abdominal distension with absent bowel sounds
- Patient cannot walk upright or walks bent over holding the abdomen

**In children specifically:**
- Irritable child who refuses to walk, jump, or be moved
- Currant-jelly stool (blood + mucus) in infant — intussusception, EMERGENCY
- Bilious vomiting in a newborn — volvulus, EMERGENCY (minutes matter)

**What the CHW Must Do.**
1. **Do NOT give antibiotics.** Appendicitis and other surgical causes need surgery, not Azithromycin or Amoxicillin. Antibiotics mask symptoms and delay diagnosis.
2. **Do NOT give food or water** (patient may need surgery — NPO)
3. **Pain management:** Paracetamol is acceptable while waiting for transport. Do NOT give NSAIDs (bleeding risk if perforation).
4. **REFER URGENTLY** to a hospital with surgical capacity.
5. **Position of comfort:** Let the patient choose (usually lying still with knees drawn up)
6. **Monitor for shock:** pale, cold, fast pulse, low BP → treat shock per protocol and REFER IMMEDIATELY

**Differential — What It Might Be (for CHW awareness, NOT diagnosis).**
- **Appendicitis:** RLQ pain, fever, vomiting, anorexia. Most common in ages 10-19.
- **Intussusception:** infant 6-36 months, episodic severe crying, currant-jelly stool. EMERGENCY.
- **Volvulus:** newborn, bilious vomiting, distension. EMERGENCY (bowel dies within hours).
- **Typhoid perforation:** endemic areas, fever >7 days, then sudden severe pain. Surgical emergency.
- **Ectopic pregnancy:** adolescent/young woman, missed period, lower abdominal pain, bleeding. EMERGENCY.
- **Ovarian torsion:** sudden severe unilateral lower abdominal pain in female, nausea/vomiting.

**Key Rule: Pain + Vomiting + Fever in the Abdomen = REFER. Do NOT treat with antibiotics and send home.**
