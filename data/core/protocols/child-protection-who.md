# WHO: Child Maltreatment — Recognition, Safe Interview, Referral

Also known as: maltrato infantil, abuso infantil, non-accidental trauma
(NAT), child abuse, safeguarding, protección infantil. Covers physical
abuse, neglect, and the pattern-recognition signals a CHW must never
miss. Sexual abuse has its own referral pathway (always refer).

**This is the most delicate clinical scenario a CHW handles.** The
medical injury is often minor compared with the safeguarding risk of
sending the child back to the abuser. **When in doubt, REFER — never
discharge to the suspected caregiver.**

**Injury red flags — suspect maltreatment when you see ANY of these:**
- Bruises in protected areas: inner thighs, buttocks, genitals, back,
  ears, neck, torso. Accidental bruises cluster on shins, elbows,
  foreheads, and bony prominences — NOT on protected, padded areas.
- **TEN-4-FACESp rule (Pierce et al. 2021)**: any bruising in
  **T**orso / **E**ars / **N**eck in a child ≤4 years, any bruise in
  a non-mobile infant (<4 months), or any bruise on the **F**renulum,
  **A**ngle of the jaw, **C**heek (fleshy), **E**yelids,
  **S**ubconjunctiva, or any **p**atterned bruise at any age. 97 %
  positive predictive value for abuse.
- **Multiple bruises in different stages of healing** (yellow/green
  old, purple/red fresh) — repeated trauma, not a single accident.
- Patterned marks: hand slap, belt, cord, bite marks, cigarette
  burns, stocking/glove burns, linear whip marks.
- **Burns — non-accidental patterns (forced immersion / inflicted):**
  Bilateral symmetrical burns (both hands, both feet, buttocks),
  sharp demarcation lines ("stocking" or "glove" distribution up to
  wrist or ankle), NO splash marks (accidental scalds have irregular
  borders and splashes), burns on protected areas (buttocks, perineum,
  soles), cigarette-shaped circular burns. Any burn in a child with
  these features is ABUSE until proven otherwise — treat the burn AND
  refer urgently for safeguarding. Do NOT discharge to the caregiver.
- **Sentinel injuries in pre-verbal infants, babies, lactantes, and
  newborns**: any bruise, bruise on the ear (moretón en la oreja),
  frenulum tear (frenillo), cheek bruise, eyelid bruise, any burn,
  any subconjunctival haemorrhage, any unexplained skin mark
  whatsoever. A baby who cannot roll over cannot injure itself — so
  ONE unexplained injury in an infant is ALWAYS a medical emergency
  AND a safeguarding emergency, no matter how minor it looks. "Only
  a small bruise" / "solo un moretoncito" in a baby is the phrase
  that gets infants killed by the next injury. Refer every single
  case — zero exceptions.

**History red flags:**
- Explanation inconsistent with the injury pattern (e.g. "fell from
  a tree" but bruises on inner thighs and back; falls produce
  bruises on exposed, bony areas).
- Mechanism incompatible with child's developmental stage ("rolled
  off the bed" in a 2-month-old who cannot roll).
- Changing story across retellings.
- Delay in seeking medical care.
- No witness, or the only witness is the suspected abuser.
- Blaming the child or a sibling for the injury.
- Multiple previous visits to different clinics or hospitals.

**Behavioural red flags in the child:**
- Quiet, withdrawn, flat affect — or the opposite, extreme clinging.
- Avoids eye contact, especially with the accompanying adult.
- **Looks at the caregiver before answering any question** (checking
  for permission to speak). This is one of the strongest signals.
- Does not react to painful procedures proportionally.
- Fearful of physical examination.

**Caregiver red flags:**
- The adult bringing the child is NOT the parent, and parents are
  "unavailable" or "at work" with no contact.
- Insists on remaining present throughout the evaluation and answers
  questions the child should answer. This is a known tactic to
  prevent disclosure.
- Hostile, evasive, or angry when questioned.
- Minimises the injury, or conversely dramatises a trivial one.
- Previous reports to child protection services.
- Unwilling to leave the child alone with the health worker.

**Immediate CHW actions — in this order.**
(1) **Treat the medical injury first.** Wound care, analgesia per
protocol (paracetamol for pain is CHW-level; opioids are not —
REFER for pain severe enough to need them).
(2) **Create a safe space to interview the child ALONE.** Find a
pretext: "I need to examine her privately — WHO guidelines." Be
firm but calm. If the caregiver refuses, that is itself a red flag
— document it and refer. Do NOT confront the caregiver verbally.
(3) **Do NOT interrogate the child.** Use open, age-appropriate,
non-leading questions: "Can you tell me how you got these marks?",
"Is there anything you want me to know?" Document the child's
answers **verbatim**, in the child's words, in quotes. Do NOT
suggest explanations.
(4) **Document meticulously.** Body diagram marking every bruise
(colour, size, location). Photograph if local protocol permits and
a trusted witness is present. Record the caregiver's story
verbatim. Note behavioural observations: "child looked at neighbour
before answering", "child did not make eye contact".
(5) **REFER URGENTLY to child protection services, social services,
or a hospital with pediatric and forensic capacity.** The referral
is non-negotiable regardless of how trivial the medical injury
looks. In many countries the CHW is a mandatory reporter.
(6) **Do NOT discharge the child to the suspected abuser.** Arrange
transport with a trusted adult, a health facility, or protection
services. If no safe alternative exists, keep the child at the
clinic under supervision until protection services arrive.
(7) **Do NOT confront, accuse, or interrogate the suspected
abuser.** Confrontation can escalate violence against the child
after discharge. Document your concerns and pass them to the
multi-disciplinary team.
(8) **Psychological first aid.** Calm environment, predictable
adults, simple reassurance. Do NOT promise secrecy — promise
safety.

**Sexual abuse — always refer.** Any suspicion of sexual abuse
(disclosure, genital injury, unexplained STI or pregnancy in a
minor, age-inappropriate sexual knowledge) is ALWAYS an urgent
referral to a specialist. Do NOT examine the genital area yourself;
that is forensic medical work. HIV PEP decisions and emergency
contraception are time-sensitive (within 72 hours) — refer fast.
**Medical red flags for sexual abuse in children:**
- Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTI) without anatomical cause
- Genital or anal pain, bleeding, discharge, or itching
- Unexplained STI at any age
- Behavioural regression (bedwetting in a toilet-trained child,
  thumb-sucking, fear of specific adults, sleep disturbances,
  sexualized play or language beyond developmental stage)
- Combination of recurrent UTI + behavioural change in a child is
  a HIGH-PRIORITY red flag — treat the UTI AND refer for safeguarding

**Neglect.** Severe malnutrition with available food at home,
untreated medical conditions, lack of immunisation, poor hygiene
beyond poverty alone, developmental delay without medical cause,
frequent school absence. Neglect is abuse. Treat, document, refer
to social services.

**What the CHW does NOT do.** You do NOT diagnose abuse — you
recognise patterns and refer. You do NOT confront the caregiver.
You do NOT promise the child that nothing bad will happen. You do
NOT send the child home with someone you suspect. You do NOT
re-interview the child multiple times — each retelling is
re-traumatisation, and the forensic interviewer needs the account
fresh.

**Mandatory reporting.** Most countries have a legal obligation on
health workers to report suspected child maltreatment to a
designated authority. Know your country's mechanism and use it.
Failure to report is often a criminal offence and always an ethical
one.

Source: WHO Clinical Handbook "Responding to child maltreatment: a clinical handbook for health professionals" (2022, ISBN 9789240048737); WHO INSPIRE: Seven Strategies for Ending Violence Against Children (2016); Pierce MC et al., "Validation of a Clinical Decision Rule to Predict Abuse in Young Children Based on Bruising Characteristics," JAMA Network Open 2021 (TEN-4-FACESp rule); WHO "Responding to children and adolescents who have been sexually abused" (2017).
