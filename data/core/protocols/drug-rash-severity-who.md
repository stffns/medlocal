# WHO: Drug Rash Severity Assessment and Management

**Scope.** A rash during or shortly after a drug course is one of the most common decision points a CHW faces. The wrong call goes two ways: stopping a harmless non-IgE eruption unnecessarily and labelling a patient "penicillin allergic" for life, or continuing a drug through the early stage of Stevens-Johnson syndrome until the patient is in a burn unit. This protocol grades drug rashes into three severity bands, each with a clear action, and lists the red flags that escalate any rash to the severe band regardless of how it looks at first.

**Mild — continue the drug with close monitoring.**
- **Appearance:** small maculopapular rash, confined to trunk or limbs, no fever, no mucous membrane involvement, no systemic symptoms, patient feels well.
- **Classic example:** non-IgE maculopapular amoxicillin rash appearing on day 5-10 of treatment. Very common, often triggered by concurrent Epstein-Barr virus (mononucleosis) rather than by a true penicillin allergy. **Labelling this patient "penicillin allergic" for life on the basis of this single rash is wrong and removes a whole class of first-line antibiotics.**
- **Action:** continue the drug if it is essential and working. Monitor the rash twice daily for spread, fever, or mucous involvement. Treat itch with oral antihistamine (cetirizine, loratadine). Document the reaction clearly and plan a formal allergy assessment at a facility when possible. Tell the caregiver exactly which red flags mean "come back urgently" — see the red flags section below.

**Moderate — stop the drug, switch class, no re-challenge without supervision.**
- **Appearance:** extensive urticaria (hives) covering a significant portion of the body, angioedema of the face or lips without throat tightness or breathing difficulty, or any rash that is spreading quickly but has none of the severe features below.
- **Action:** STOP the suspected drug. Start an alternative from a different class (see drug-allergies-alternatives protocol). Give oral antihistamine. Record the allergy prominently on the patient card. Do not re-challenge without a formal allergy assessment in a facility. Monitor for 24-48 hours for progression to severe — angioedema can evolve into airway compromise, and urticaria can be the first phase of anaphylaxis.

**Severe — STOP immediately, refer urgently, NEVER re-challenge.**
- **Anaphylaxis:** any two of wheeze, stridor, throat tightness or hoarseness, hypotension, collapse, vomiting, or generalised urticaria evolving within minutes to hours of the drug. **Give IM epinephrine immediately** (see the anaphylaxis protocol), refer urgently.
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS):** painful dusky rash with target-like lesions, **mucous membrane involvement in eyes, mouth, or genitals**, skin peeling on less than 10 % of the body surface. Fever is usually present. Drug usually started 1-4 weeks before the rash.
- **Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN):** SJS with skin detachment on more than 30 % of the body surface area. The skin looks scalded. Nikolsky sign positive (gentle lateral pressure separates the epidermis).
- **DRESS (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms):** extensive maculopapular rash appearing **2 to 6 weeks after drug start** (longer than most rashes) with fever, lymphadenopathy, facial swelling, and involvement of internal organs (liver, kidney, heart, lung). Eosinophilia on CBC if available. The combination of rash + fever + lymphadenopathy + organ dysfunction is the hallmark — this is NOT a simple rash and NOT a simple allergy.
- **Fixed drug eruption:** round, well-demarcated, dark red or violaceous patch that recurs at the same site each time the patient takes the drug. Common on lips, genitals, palms. Stop the drug permanently.
- **Action for any severe pattern:** STOP the drug immediately, refer urgently to a facility with burn-level care (for SJS/TEN) or medical admission (for DRESS and anaphylaxis after initial treatment). Document the drug, date, and reaction. **Never re-challenge** a patient who has had any of these reactions — the mortality of a second exposure is much higher than the first.

**Red flags that escalate ANY rash to severe — look for these on every drug rash.**
1. Mucous membrane involvement: eyes (conjunctivitis, pain, photophobia), mouth (ulcers, erosions, pain with swallowing), genitals (erosions, pain with urination).
2. Skin pain out of proportion to appearance, blistering, or peeling. **Nikolsky sign positive** (skin slides off with gentle lateral pressure) = emergency.
3. Fever ≥38.5 °C developing during or after an antibiotic course together with a rash.
4. Facial swelling that is spreading, or any throat tightness, voice change, or difficulty breathing.
5. Eosinophilia on complete blood count if available (≥700 cells/µL, or ≥10 % of white cells).
6. Tender or swollen lymph nodes combined with rash.
7. Rash appearing ≥2 weeks after drug start, rather than in the first days — DRESS specifically has a long latency.
8. Any organ symptoms: jaundice, dark urine, reduced urine output, unusual bruising, altered mental state, shortness of breath.

**Any one of these eight red flags promotes the rash to the severe band, regardless of how small the skin finding looks.** In doubt, refer.

**Documentation requirement.** Every drug rash, no matter how mild, must be recorded on the patient card: drug name, dose, start date, day of reaction, description of rash (including severity band), whether mucous membranes or systemic symptoms were present, action taken, and a clear re-challenge status (mild = may be re-challenged with supervisor review; moderate/severe = NEVER re-challenge). This record is what the next clinician needs to decide safely the next time the patient is sick.

**The "ask what happened" rule.** A patient who says "I am allergic to penicillin" may be describing a fatal SJS reaction or a childhood stomach ache. Always ask: (1) what exactly happened, (2) how long after starting the drug, (3) did it affect eyes, mouth, or genitals, (4) was there fever, (5) did anyone consider the reaction severe at the time. The answers determine whether penicillin is safe to consider again or must be avoided for life. Side effects (nausea, headache, diarrhoea) are not allergies and should not remove a drug from the patient's future options.

Source: WHO Essential Medicines List; WHO Model Formulary; WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children; consensus definitions from the International Consensus on Drug Allergy (2014); published SJS/TEN case definitions from the RegiSCAR registry; AAP clinical reports on paediatric drug hypersensitivity.
