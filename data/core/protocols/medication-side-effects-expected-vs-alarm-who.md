# WHO: Medication Side Effects — Expected vs Alarm Signs

**Scope.** Many CHW-administered drugs cause side effects that are benign and expected — stopping the drug causes more harm than tolerating the effect. Other side effects look similar but signal serious toxicity and require immediate discontinuation. CHWs must be able to tell the two apart, reassure patients about the first, and react to the second. When in doubt, refer — never continue a drug if the patient has any alarm sign on this list.

**Expected — reassure and continue the drug.**
- **Rifampicin:** orange-red urine, tears, sweat, saliva, and permanent staining of soft contact lenses. Tell every TB patient about this BEFORE starting treatment so the colour change is not a reason to stop. Mild nausea in the first week is also expected.
- **Oral iron (ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate):** black or dark stool, constipation, nausea, metallic taste, mild epigastric discomfort. Take with food if intolerant, increase fluids and fibre, do NOT stop the iron. Black stool from iron is not gastrointestinal bleeding.
- **Metronidazole:** metallic taste, darker urine, nausea. No alcohol during treatment and for 48 hours after — alcohol causes a disulfiram reaction (flushing, vomiting, palpitations).
- **Isoniazid:** mild tingling in hands and feet (peripheral neuropathy). Give pyridoxine (vitamin B6) 10-25 mg daily as prophylaxis, especially in pregnancy, HIV, malnutrition, diabetes.
- **Chloroquine:** mild generalised itching, especially in patients with darker skin. Not an allergy, does not require stopping. Antihistamine if troublesome.
- **Artemether-lumefantrine (ACT):** mild dizziness, nausea, headache, sleep disturbance in the first 24-48 hours. Take with fatty food.
- **Cotrimoxazole:** mild nausea, soft stool, small maculopapular rash in the first 2 weeks. Watch for progression (see alarm section).
- **Amoxicillin / ampicillin:** loose stool, yeast vaginitis or oral thrush. Probiotic foods (yogurt) help. A mild late rash on day 5-10 is often a non-IgE maculopapular eruption, frequently EBV-associated, not a lifelong penicillin allergy — see drug-rash severity protocol.
- **Salbutamol (inhaled):** fine tremor, palpitations, mild anxiety, headache. All transient and dose-related.
- **Paracetamol at therapeutic dose:** essentially none. Any nausea, jaundice, or right upper quadrant pain is an alarm sign.

**Alarm — STOP the drug and refer urgently.**
- **Jaundice (yellow eyes or skin) on any TB drug:** isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide can all cause drug-induced hepatitis. **STOP ALL TB drugs** and refer for liver function tests. Do not restart without specialist guidance.
- **Visual changes on ethambutol** (blurred vision, trouble distinguishing red from green, reduced acuity): optic neuritis. STOP ethambutol immediately and refer — the visual loss can be irreversible if the drug is continued.
- **Severe or spreading rash with any of: fever >38.5 °C, mucous membrane involvement (mouth, eyes, genitals), skin pain or peeling, facial swelling, eosinophilia, or lymphadenopathy:** possible Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, or DRESS. STOP the suspected drug, refer urgently, never re-challenge. See the drug-rash severity protocol for the full decision tree.
- **Bleeding (black tarry stool, coffee-ground vomitus, nose, gums, unusual bruising) on aspirin or any NSAID:** gastrointestinal ulcer or coagulopathy. STOP the NSAID, refer.
- **Deafness, ringing in ears, or reduced urine output during prolonged aminoglycoside therapy (gentamicin, amikacin, streptomycin) beyond 5-7 days:** ototoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Refer for alternatives, do not extend the course without specialist supervision.
- **Severe watery diarrhea appearing during or after an antibiotic course:** possible *Clostridioides difficile* colitis. STOP the antibiotic if possible, rehydrate with ORS, refer.
- **Psychiatric changes on efavirenz or isoniazid:** vivid nightmares, confusion, suicidal thoughts. Do not dismiss, refer.
- **Lactic acidosis signs on prolonged stavudine or metformin** (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, fast deep breathing, extreme fatigue): medical emergency, STOP the drug, refer.

**Pediatric-specific adverse reactions — know these by heart.**
- **Metoclopramide in children <1 year:** **CONTRAINDICATED**. Acute extrapyramidal reactions (oculogyric crisis, trismus, torticollis, dystonia) are well documented and the FDA limits metoclopramide in young children for this reason. If dystonia occurs, stop the drug and refer; IV diphenhydramine can reverse the reaction in a facility.
- **Chloramphenicol in neonates (<1 month):** **CONTRAINDICATED** outside very narrow indications. Grey baby syndrome: hypothermia, abdominal distension, cyanosis, cardiovascular collapse, death. If a CHW sees a newborn deteriorating on chloramphenicol, this is the differential.
- **Aspirin in any child <12 years:** Reye syndrome risk if the child has a viral illness (influenza, varicella). Use paracetamol or — with the age cautions below — ibuprofen instead.
- **Ibuprofen in infants <6 months:** avoid. Under 3 months it is contraindicated.
- **Gentamicin in neonates and young children:** dose precisely by weight, monitor for hearing and renal function if the course exceeds 5-7 days, never combine with other ototoxic drugs without supervision.
- **Tetracyclines in children <8 years and in pregnancy after week 15:** permanent tooth staining and enamel hypoplasia. Use alternatives.
- **Fluoroquinolones in children:** concerns about cartilage and tendon toxicity. Reserve for situations where no safer alternative exists, and only under specialist guidance.
- **Ceftriaxone in neonates with calcium-containing IV fluids (Ringer's lactate, parenteral nutrition):** ceftriaxone-calcium precipitates can cause fatal pulmonary and renal complications in neonates. Use cefotaxime instead, or separate the calcium infusion strictly.
- **Codeine and tramadol in children <12 years:** avoid. Ultra-rapid metabolisers can develop life-threatening respiratory depression. Codeine is also contraindicated in breastfeeding mothers of neonates for the same reason.

**What to ask the patient at every follow-up.**
Have you noticed any of: yellow eyes or skin, severe rash or blisters, sores in your mouth or eyes, vision changes, severe stomach pain, unusual bruising or bleeding, very dark urine, hearing changes or ringing in ears, shortness of breath, or severe vomiting? If the answer is yes to any, stop the drug and refer.

**The underlying rule.** Expected side effects are discomforts that pass or can be managed without stopping the drug. Alarm signs are system-level failures (liver, kidney, skin, bone marrow, nervous system) that get worse as long as the drug continues. When a patient is uncomfortable, reassure. When a patient looks sick from the drug, stop and refer.

Source: WHO Model Formulary (2023); WHO Essential Medicines List (2023); WHO/UNICEF IMCI Guidelines; WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2013, 2nd ed.); AAP Red Book and clinical reports on paediatric adverse drug reactions; FDA Drug Safety Communications on metoclopramide, codeine, and ceftriaxone-calcium.
