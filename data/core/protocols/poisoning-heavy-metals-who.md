# WHO: Heavy Metal Poisoning — Lead, Mercury, Arsenic

Also known as: intoxicación por metales pesados, plomo (Pb), mercurio
(Hg), arsénico (As), saturnismo. Heavy-metal exposure is a chronic
public-health problem in mining, smelting, informal recycling, and
contaminated-water communities. The CHW role is to **suspect, identify
the source, eliminate exposure, and REFER** — chelation treatment
(DMSA, EDTA, BAL, dimercaprol) is hospital-level only.

**When to suspect lead poisoning.** Children: developmental delay,
irritability, recurrent abdominal pain ("lead colic"), unexplained
anaemia, learning problems, pica (eating non-food). Adults:
hypertension, peripheral neuropathy (wrist/foot drop), abdominal
colic, anaemia, blue gum line ("Burton line") in chronic cases.
**Acute encephalopathy** — vomiting, convulsions, lethargy, coma →
medical emergency. WHO action threshold for blood lead is 5 µg/dL,
but there is **no safe level** in children — CDC lowered its
reference value to 3.5 µg/dL in 2021.

**Lead exposure clues — ask every patient:** lead-based paint in old
housing; lead-glazed pottery used for food or drink; informal battery
recycling; artisanal mining or smelting; soldering or stained-glass
work; leaded petrol (still used in some countries); traditional
remedies (azarcón, greta, some Ayurvedic preparations); cosmetics
(kohl, surma); contaminated soil near factories or busy roads.

**When to suspect mercury poisoning.** Vapour exposure (acute):
metal-fume fever, cough, shortness of breath, chest pain. Chronic:
fine tremor, insomnia, memory loss, personality change ("erethism"),
gingivitis, metallic taste, paraesthesias, kidney damage.
Methylmercury (from fish): peripheral and central neurotoxicity, in
utero brain damage. Mercury exposure clues: artisanal gold mining
(Hg–Au amalgam burning is the largest single source globally),
broken thermometers and fluorescent tubes, skin-lightening creams,
dental amalgam work, large predator fish in subsistence fishing
communities.

**When to suspect arsenic poisoning.** Acute: severe abdominal pain,
vomiting, profuse watery or bloody diarrhoea, hypotension, cardiac
arrhythmias — can mimic cholera. Chronic: hyperpigmentation
(raindrop pattern), palmar/plantar hyperkeratosis, peripheral
neuropathy, skin/bladder/lung cancer, Bowen's disease. Arsenic
exposure clues: tube-well groundwater in known endemic areas
(Bangladesh, eastern India, Argentina, Chile, Mexico, parts of the
US Southwest and Vietnam); old pesticides; some traditional
medicines.

**Immediate CHW actions — universal for any heavy-metal exposure:**
(1) **Identify and remove the source** — this is the single most
important intervention. Stop further exposure today. (2) Remove
contaminated clothing; wash skin with soap and large amounts of
water if dermal exposure. (3) Do **NOT** induce vomiting. Do **NOT**
attempt chelation in the field. (4) For **acute ingestion or
encephalopathy: REFER URGENTLY** — these need IV access, blood
metal levels, and supervised chelation. (5) For **chronic
suspicion: refer routinely** for blood/urine testing and follow-up.
(6) For **acute mercury vapour exposure: ventilate the area, move
the patient to fresh air, do NOT vacuum spilled elemental mercury**
(it aerosolises) — refer for hazmat handling.

**Special populations.** **Children** are far more vulnerable to
neurodevelopmental harm at any blood level — refer any suspicion
even without symptoms. **Pregnant women**: lead, mercury and
arsenic all cross the placenta and harm fetal brain development;
refer for testing and source elimination. **Breastfeeding mothers**
exposed to lead or mercury: continue breastfeeding (benefits
outweigh the risk in most cases) but eliminate the source urgently
and discuss with the supervising clinician.

**Community prevention — the highest-impact CHW action.** Identify
and report community-level sources: lead-painted schools or homes,
contaminated water sources, artisanal mining sites, lead-glazed
pottery vendors, skin-lightening cream sales. Educate families:
do not use traditional metal remedies, do not store food in
artisanal glazed pottery, use protective equipment in mining and
soldering, test groundwater in endemic areas, choose smaller
non-predator fish. Advocate for childhood blood-lead screening
programmes where lead exposure is prevalent.

Source: WHO Lead Poisoning and Health fact sheet (updated 2024); WHO Guidelines on Clinical Management of Exposure to Lead (2021); WHO Mercury and Health fact sheet (2024); WHO Exposure to Arsenic: A Major Public Health Concern (2022); WHO/IPCS INTOX Programme.
